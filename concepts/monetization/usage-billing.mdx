title: 'Usage billing'

Most pricing is a flat monthly fee. Usage billing is the other half: your customer pays for what they consume, and you decide where that stops.

Kerne splits that into **three separate decisions**. They look similar and they are not, so it's worth being precise once:

<CardGroup cols={3}>
  <Card title="What's included" icon="gift">
    Free every period. `720` minutes, `10,000` calls, `1,000,000` tokens.
  </Card>
  <Card title="What it costs after" icon="receipt">
    A rate you write once, and an optional **ceiling in money**.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Where it stops dead" icon="hand">
    A ceiling in **units**, which actually blocks. Optional, and rarely what you want.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

The one that surprises people: **the money ceiling doesn't block anything.** Past it, your customer keeps working and you stop charging. That's deliberate — see [Why the cap doesn't block](#why-the-cap-doesnt-block).

## Four steps

A makerspace rents out its laser cutters. Their offer: **0.35 € per hour of cutter time, never more than 120 € a month per customer.**

<Steps>
  <Step title="Say what you count">
    A **quota** feature: unit `minute`, renewing **every billing cycle**.

    Meter in the smallest unit you bill — minutes, not hours. Kerne counts in whole numbers, which is what makes concurrent consumption safe, so a fractional hour has nowhere to go.

    A billed quota has to restart at zero each period. If unused allowance carried over, last period's consumption would be billable again this period — so "carries over" is unavailable on a feature some plan bills, rather than allowed to do that quietly.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Set the rate">
    Open that feature's cell on the plan, and choose to keep serving past the allowance and charge for it. Then write the rate the way you sell it — an amount and a quantity: **0.35 EUR per 60 minutes**.

    Kerne creates the meter and the price at Stripe from that one line. There is no id to copy back, and nothing to configure on the other side.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Say where it stops">
    Nothing is included here, so the allowance is `0` minutes. The spending ceiling is `120` EUR per billing cycle.

    Leave that ceiling empty for an add-on meant to scale freely.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Count it from your backend">
    ```typescript
    await kerne.consume('cutter_minutes', {
      subject: customerId,
      delta: 60, // one hour
    });
    ```
  </Step>
</Steps>

Kerne counts, and a background worker hands the billable minutes to Stripe. Your customer's invoice grows to 120 € and stops.

<Note>
  Usage billing runs on Stripe today. On Polar you get an explicit refusal instead of an entitlement that looks configured and never charges — meter the plans that need it on Stripe.
</Note>

## The same four steps, five businesses

| What's sold | Counted in | The rate | Where it stops |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Laser-cutter time | `minute` | 0.35 EUR per 60 minutes, a started hour counts in full | never past 120 EUR a month |
| Model calls | `token` | 2.00 EUR per 1,000,000 tokens, billed to the exact token | 1,000,000 included, then billed |
| Transactional SMS | `message` | 0.04 EUR per message | 500 included, cut off at 20,000 |
| Object storage | `gigabyte` | 0.10 EUR per GB, 0.06 EUR past 100 GB | 100 GB included |
| AI credits sold per seat | `credit`, per `user` | 0.01 EUR per credit | 1,000 free per user, never past 20 EUR per user |

Only the last row needs something the others don't: it counts per `user` rather than per customer, which is [dimensions](#per-machine-per-project-per-seat).

## The rate

A rate is **an amount and the quantity it buys** — "0.35 EUR per 60 minutes", not "0.0058 EUR per minute". Kerne keeps both halves, because 0.35 divided by 60 has no exact decimal and a flattened rate is unrecognisable as the offer you sell.

Past that, two questions. Their answers are independent, and between them they cover every shape you can build.

### Does a partly-used quantity count in full?

1,500 units at 5.00 EUR per 1,000:

| You charge | You invoice |
| --- | --- |
| **for every 1,000 started** | 10.00 EUR — the second thousand counts whole |
| **for the exact usage** | 7.50 EUR — the exact fraction |

The editor answers it back as you type: a sample quantity, what it costs, and — when a started quantity counts whole — the fact that one unit less costs exactly the same.

### Does the rate change as usage grows?

Add a tier — "past 10,000 units, 4.00 EUR per 1,000" — then say what a tier applies to. A customer who used 20,000 pays:

| Each tier is priced | You invoice |
| --- | --- |
| **on its own slice** | 90.00 EUR — the first 10,000 stay at their own rate |
| **at whatever rate the total reaches** | 80.00 EUR — the reached rate reprices the lot |

Nothing here assumes the rate falls as usage grows. Volume discounts and congestion pricing are both real offers; a tier just says what applies past a point, in either direction.

<Note>
  The two questions combine. "0.35 EUR per 60 minutes, every started hour whole, dropping to 0.28 EUR past 150 hours" is one rate — Kerne does the quantity arithmetic before the provider ever sees a number, which is what lets a sold-by quantity and a tier schedule coexist. Stripe cannot express that pairing on its own.
</Note>

## Per machine, per project, per seat

The example above pools every cutter into one counter. Often that's exactly right — the customer is billed for total consumption, and which cutter produced it is their business.

Sometimes it isn't. **Dimensions** let one quota split into several counters under the same customer:

```typescript
await kerne.consume('cutter_minutes', {
  subject: customerId,
  dimension: 'cutter_42', // ← this
  delta: 60,
});
```

Declare what a feature is counted by (`minute` per `cutter`) on the **feature**, then each plan chooses what that means:

| Allowance | What your customer gets |
| --- | --- |
| **Shared across every cutter** | One pool. 200 hours total, whoever burns them. |
| **Each cutter gets its own** | 200 hours *per cutter*, counted separately. Cutter A running out doesn't touch cutter B. |

And the cap has its own, independent choice — because these two promise opposite things:

| Cap | What you're promising |
| --- | --- |
| **Total for the customer** | "Your bill never exceeds 120 € a period." Reassuring for them, and it caps what you earn however much they grow. |
| **Per cutter** | "Each cutter costs you at most 120 €." Ten cutters can bill ten times it — and the cost of adding one more is known in advance, on both sides. |

The second one is the one people forget exists, and it's usually the better default: it's how you sell 1,000 AI credits per user with a 20 € ceiling per user, 100 GB per project at 50 € per project, or a white-label deal where the agency knows what one more of their own clients costs them.

<Note>
  Allowance and cap bind independently: "shared pool, one cap" and "own allowance per cutter, own cap per cutter" are both valid, and so are the two mixes.
</Note>

Attribution works either way: even on a shared pool, every usage event records which cutter it came from, so "which one cost me the most" stays answerable.

## Why the cap doesn't block

This is the part worth understanding, because it's the opposite of what most systems do.

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Money and access are separate" icon="scale-balanced">
    Hitting a spending cap means *you* stop charging. Your customer notices nothing. Their cutters keep running, their API keeps answering.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Blocking is its own setting" icon="hand">
    If you genuinely want consumption to stop, that's the usage limit — a ceiling in units, sitting one rung below on the same rule, and empty by default.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

The reason is simple: a spending cap is a promise you made about *your invoice*. Turning it into an outage would punish the customer for a promise you made to reassure them.

## What you see

The **Usage billing** screen shows every subscriber on a billed entitlement: consumed, billable, already charged, and how much room is left under the cap.

It also shows two things nothing else would tell you:

- **Capped** — still consuming, no longer charged. Working as designed, but you want to know.
- **Not billing** — the provider refused this entitlement's usage (its price was archived, its meter was turned off). Access still works, revenue is silently zero. This is the failure this screen exists for.

<Note>
  The numbers on that screen come from the same function the billing worker uses. What you read is what gets invoiced, not a parallel estimate that can drift.
</Note>

## Things that will bite you

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Meter in the smallest unit, always">
    Minutes, not hours. Megabytes, not gigabytes, unless you never sell a fraction of one. `delta` is a whole number — that's what makes concurrent consumption safe — so a fractional hour has nowhere to go. Same discipline as storing money in cents.

    The rate then puts the offer back: you count minutes and sell "0.35 EUR per 60 minutes".
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Repricing everything can make a bigger bill smaller">
    If you priced tiers at whatever rate the total reaches, crossing one can *lower* the total: at 5 € then 4 € per 1,000, ten thousand units cost 50 € and ten thousand and one cost 44 €.

    That's the pricing working as designed, and your spending cap still holds — it stops a customer just short of a tier their cap can't afford to enter, rather than letting them jump into it. The rate editor says so when you pick a tier that really is cheaper than the one before it.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="A cap counts whole quantities, it does not divide">
    A 120 € cap at 0.35 € per started hour doesn't buy 342.8 hours. It buys **342 whole hours**: the 343rd would count whole and invoice 120.05 €. Kerne computes it the way the invoice will.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="The cap resets on the feature's period, not on the invoice">
    A 120 € spending cap means 120 € **per renewal period** — the one you set on the feature, not the one you bill on. Usage is always invoiced monthly, so the two only line up when the feature renews on the **billing cycle**.

    | Feature renews | One monthly invoice can carry |
    | --- | --- |
    | Billing cycle | 120 € — they match |
    | Calendar month | up to 240 €, since invoices run from the customer's own renewal date and can straddle two calendar months |
    | Weekly | about 4 × 120 € |
    | Daily | about 30 × 120 € |

    None of these is wrong — a daily quota that bills is a perfectly good design. But if you meant "their bill never exceeds 120 €", set the feature to renew on the **billing cycle**. Kerne names the period in force above the entitlement and puts the multiplier next to the cap when they don't match.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Changing a rate means replacing it">
    A price is immutable on its amount at the provider, so "change the rate" is always "create another one and point this entitlement at it" — which is what the editor does when you replace a rate, and why it says so. The meter itself is reused, not recreated, so the consumption history behind it stays whole.

    Customers already subscribed keep the rate they signed up on: their subscription still carries the price it was created with. Changing their plan brings them onto the current one, since a plan change reconciles the whole subscription against the target plan.

    The replaced rate stays at Stripe, where it keeps showing up whenever you pick one. Archive it from that list once nothing points at it — Kerne refuses while an entitlement still bills on it, and names which. "Archive" rather than "delete" because Stripe keeps every price that has ever existed: the action hides it, it does not remove it.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="A price Kerne didn't create can't carry a cap">
    A spending cap is inverted into a unit ceiling — "how many hours fit under 120 €?" — so Kerne has to be able to price your usage without asking the provider. It can do that for any rate written here, schedules included.

    What it won't do is guess. Point an entitlement at a price built at the provider with something Kerne has no equivalent for — a flat fee attached to a tier, say — and it says so rather than inventing a shape. That price still bills perfectly well; it just can't be capped.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="An unlimited allowance and a billed overage contradict each other">
    Unlimited means everything is included, so nothing is ever billable: the entitlement would carry a rate, a cap, a meter, and charge zero forever. Kerne refuses the pair instead of storing it.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="If the metered price gets archived at the provider">
    Existing subscribers keep billing on it, but nobody new can subscribe to that plan. Kerne flags the entitlement as **Not billing** so you find out from your dashboard rather than from a support ticket.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>
