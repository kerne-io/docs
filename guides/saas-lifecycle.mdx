---
title: "Lifecycle: from waitlist to scale"
description: "Which Kerne features fit each stage"
---

A SaaS doesn't need the same access model on day one and at scale. Kerne is built so you move through these stages by changing configuration and a couple of feature keys - not by re-architecting how your app checks access.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Stage 1: Validation">
    You don't have a product worth registering for yet - you have an idea worth measuring interest in.

    <Info>
    **Goal:** collect real emails before writing a signup flow, a pricing page, or any billing code.

    **Kerne strategy:** close registration entirely and run a waitlist instead. Zero billing setup required at this stage.
    </Info>

    ```typescript
    await kerne.waitlist.join({ email: 'early-adopter@example.com' });
    ```

    See [Registration modes](/concepts/identity/registration-modes).
  </Step>

  <Step title="Stage 2: Private beta">
    You have something worth trying, but not worth a public signup button yet - you want to control exactly who gets in and in what order.

    <Info>
    **Goal:** admit a small, chosen cohort (a launch list, people who asked first) without opening the doors.

    **Kerne strategy:** switch to invite-only. Generate codes for specific cohorts, and admit waitlist entries by hand as you're ready for them - see [Implement invite-only SaaS](/guides/auth/implement-invite-only-saas).
    </Info>
  </Step>

  <Step title="Stage 3: Self-serve">
    The product is ready for anyone to sign up and pay without you in the loop.

    <Info>
    **Goal:** let strangers subscribe and start using premium features immediately, with zero manual steps on your end.

    **Kerne strategy:** open registration, set up plans and checkout (see [Subscription setup](/guides/getting-started/subscription-setup)), and gate premium features by entitlement, not by plan name - a plan can be renamed or restructured later without breaking every check.
    </Info>

    ```tsx
    import { Access } from '@kerne/react';

    <Access featureKey="advanced_analytics" fallback={<UpgradePrompt />}>
      <AnalyticsDashboard />
    </Access>
    ```
  </Step>

  <Step title="Stage 4: Negotiated deals">
    A customer wants terms that don't match any plan you sell publicly - a bigger limit, a custom discount, a handshake deal.

    <Info>
    **Goal:** give one customer a non-standard limit without inventing a one-off plan just for them.

    **Kerne strategy:** use an [entitlement override](/sdks/server/admin/overrides) (`kerne.admin.grant()`) - it's the same mechanism as a normal plan limit, just scoped to that one customer instead of everyone on a plan.
    </Info>

    Not built yet if you need it at this stage: SSO, billing a group of users as one subject, and a first-class "enterprise contract" object - see [Subject: the concept that decides fit](/concepts/overview#subject-the-concept-that-decides-fit) for the current boundary.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Stage 5: The deal has to change">
    Sooner or later you want to sell something different from what you sell today - a higher price, a smaller allowance, a feature moved to a pricier tier. The moment that decision is made, a second one hides right behind it: does everyone who already bought the old deal get moved onto the new one, or do they keep what they signed up for?

    Both answers are ordinary, and you've been on both sides of them without thinking about it. A magazine renews you at the price you first paid, even years later. A phone carrier usually lets existing customers keep their old plan when it launches a new lineup, and only new customers see the new prices. A landlord can't raise a sitting tenant's rent just because new tenants pay more. On the other hand, a car insurer reprices everyone at renewal based on this year's risk, and a security fix has to reach every customer immediately, old and new alike, no exceptions and no opt-out.

    None of these is more "correct" than the others. The right one depends on what you promised when the customer bought in, and why the deal is changing now. A price going up for new customers only is a business decision; being able to answer "what happens to the people already in?" on purpose, instead of by accident, is the part worth being deliberate about.

    <Info>
    **Goal:** decide, for each change you make, whether it reaches people who already bought in or only the ones who sign up after - and have that be a choice, not a side effect of how you happened to make the change.

    **Kerne strategy:** what a plan includes is versioned automatically. A subscriber is pinned to the version they signed up under, so publishing a change only ever reaches new subscribers by default - existing ones keep exactly what they had until you explicitly move them. See [grandfathering](/concepts/monetization/products-and-plans#why-pack-exists-grandfathering-by-default) for how that pinning works.
    </Info>
  </Step>
</Steps>
